Moving escalators and horizontal moving walkways are ubiquitous in transit stations, airports, and multi-story shopping centers.

Behind the rhythmic clatter of grooved aluminum steps lies an industrial electric drive motor connected to heavy steel chains, delivering immense continuous rotational torque.

When loose fabric, shoelaces, or soft rubber footwear contact the interface between moving steps and stationary plates, mechanical entrapment can escalate in seconds.

### The Comb Plate Pinch Point: Why the Motor Won't Stall

At the top and bottom landings of every escalator sits a stationary metal tooth comb (_the comb plate_). The teeth of the comb interlock precisely with the longitudinal cleats of the aluminum steps to smoothly transfer passengers onto the floor.

- **The Pinch Geometry**: Over time, mechanical wear, heavy baggage impacts, or foreign debris can create millimeter-wide gaps between the moving step cleats and the comb teeth.
- **The Torque Trap**: An industrial escalator motor produces thousands of Newton-meters of torque designed to lift tons of human payload up vertical inclines. The motor cannot "feel" a shoelace, scarf, or toddler’s finger. It will draw clothing into the internal mechanical sprocket without slowing down.
- **The Physical Remedy**: Attempting to play tug-of-war with an active escalator motor is futile. The **only** effective intervention is immediately cutting electrical power by slamming the **Emergency Stop button**.

### The Skirt Panel Friction Trap

A secondary major entrapment zone exists along the stationary vertical side walls (_skirt panels_):

- Soft, high-friction foam clogs and rubber boots easily grip against the stationary side panel.
- As the step moves downward or forward, the rotational friction curls the rubber shoe into the 3 to 4 mm gap between the step and the skirt.
- **The Preventive Rule**: Always stand in the center of the step, strictly between the yellow boundary lines, and never brush footwear against the side skirt brushes.
