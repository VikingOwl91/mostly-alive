The human brain consumes roughly twenty percent of the body's entire oxygen supply despite occupying only two percent of its total mass. To keep this demanding neural apparatus functioning, arterial blood is pumped upward through high-pressure cerebral highways.

An acute stroke occurs when one of these highways suffers an abrupt mechanical failure: either a blood clot jams an artery (ischemic stroke, roughly 85% of cases), or a vessel ruptures and leaks under pressure directly into cranial tissue (hemorrhagic stroke, roughly 15% of cases). In both events, neurons deprived of oxygen begin perishing within minutes.

### The FAST Protocol

Clinical studies demonstrate that three simple physical tests identify over 85% of acute anterior circulation strokes:

1. **Face**: Ask for a smile. When the motor cortex or facial nerve pathway loses perfusion, the muscles on one side of the face lose tone, resulting in a distinct, involuntary droop of the eyelid or corner of the mouth.
2. **Arms**: Ask the person to close their eyes and extend both arms forward at shoulder level for ten seconds. When unilateral motor pathways fail, the brain can no longer maintain symmetric motor output against gravity, causing the affected arm to drift downward, pronate (rotate inward), or remain entirely limp.
3. **Speech**: Ask for a simple repetition. Is the articulation slurred (dysarthria), or is the person unable to find words or understand spoken language (aphasia)?

### Additional Neurological Warning Signs

While FAST covers the vast majority of cases, posterior circulation strokes may present with:

- Sudden severe loss of balance or uncoordinated ataxia.
- Sudden loss of vision in one eye or double vision (diplopia).
- Sudden severe "thunderclap" headache with no prior history.
- Sudden numbness or sensory loss across one side of the face or body.

### Why Time and Zero Oral Intake Matter

Modern stroke therapy relies on intravenous thrombolytic medications (to dissolve clots) and endovascular thrombectomy (mechanical catheter retrieval of large vessel occlusions). Both interventions are strictly time-bound: tissue salvage decays rapidly past the first few hours from symptom onset. Recording the exact minute the patient was last known to be completely normal gives emergency physicians the clinical baseline required to initiate treatment safely.

Furthermore, strokes frequently impair the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerve reflexes that control the epiglottis. Giving a stroke patient a glass of water, food, or aspirin pills frequently routes the liquid straight into the lungs, turning a treatable stroke into a fatal aspiration emergency.
