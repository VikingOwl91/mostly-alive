Exposure to sub-zero environmental temperatures, sub-zero wind chills, or direct contact with cryogenic liquefied gases causes freezing of skin and deeper subcutaneous tissues (_frostbite_).

At tissue temperatures below $-0.55^\circ\text{C}$ ($31^\circ\text{F}$), extracellular water forms ice crystals. As water freezes out of the extracellular matrix, osmotic gradients draw intracellular water out of cells, causing severe cellular dehydration, hyperosmolality, and irreversible membrane disruption.

### The Refreezing Catastrophe: The Wilderness Rule

In mountain rescue and wilderness medicine, there is one non-negotiable rule regarding frostbite: **Never thaw frozen tissue if there is any chance it will freeze again.**

- **The Freeze-Thaw-Refreeze Cycle**: When tissue freezes once, some cellular damage occurs. When it thaws, capillary blood flow returns with severe inflammatory swelling. If this thawed, edematous tissue freezes a second time, internal microvascular thrombosis is total and absolute.
- **The Protocol**: If a climber with frozen toes is still miles from civilization, it is far better for them to walk out on rigid, frozen feet than to thaw them in a tent, refreeze on the descent, and lose the entire foot to gangrene.

### The Snow-Rubbing Myth: Mechanical Cryo-Abrasion

For centuries, folklore in cold climates advised people to "rub frozen fingers with snow."

From a biophysical perspective, **rubbing frozen tissue with snow is devastating**:

- Frostbitten tissue is filled with sharp, jagged micro-ice crystals.
- When you rub the skin with snow or massage it with your hands, you press those sharp internal ice crystals against delicate microvascular capillary beds, slicing cell walls and nerve sheaths like razor blades.
- Cold, wet snow further cools the surface and abrades the epidermis.

### The Rapid Water Bath Standard (37°C to 39°C)

Once in a safe, permanently warm environment:

1. **The Water Bath**: Immerse the affected extremities in warm, circulating water strictly maintained at **37°C to 39°C (98.6°F to 102.2°F)**.
2. **Why Dry Heat Burns**: Never use dry heat (radiators, stoves, hot blowers, campfires). Frostbitten tissue is completely anesthetic (numb). The patient cannot feel heat and will literally cook their flesh into severe third-degree burns without feeling a thing.
3. **Post-Thaw Care**: Expect intense, agonizing pain during rewarming as perfusion returns. Air dry gently, place sterile gauze between digits to prevent maceration, and transport to a specialized burn or trauma center for advanced microvascular therapy.
